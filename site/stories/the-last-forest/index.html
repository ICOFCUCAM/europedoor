The last forest that was never cut

Białowieża has never been logged, which in Europe makes it a survivor of something the rest of the continent lost quietly and without noticing.

Every other forest in Europe has been cut down at least once. That is the fact to hold onto, because it makes the exception intelligible: Białowieża, on the Polish-Belarusian border, was a royal hunting reserve, then a tsarist one, and being reserved for kings turned out to be the only conservation policy that worked for six hundred years.

What that produces is not what people expect a forest to look like. There is no clean floor and no cathedral of trunks. There are dead trees standing where they died, dead trees lying where they fell, and a quantity of fungus and beetle life that has nowhere else to go. About a quarter of the biomass in a primeval forest is dead wood, and roughly a fifth of all forest species depend on it. A managed forest removes exactly that portion, which is why a managed forest looks tidy and holds a fraction of the life.

The bison are the reason most visitors come, and the bison are also a lesson in how narrowly this was saved. The last wild European bison in Białowieża was shot in 1921. The species survived only because fifty-four animals existed in zoos, and every bison alive today descends from twelve of them. They were reintroduced here from 1929 and there are now several hundred on the Polish side.

The strict reserve — the part that has genuinely never been managed — can be entered only with a licensed guide, in small groups, on marked paths. This annoys people who feel they have travelled a long way. It is also the only reason there is anything left to travel to.

The border runs through it. Since 2021 a wall has run along that border, built in response to a migration crisis, straight through a UNESCO World Heritage site and across the routes that bison, lynx and wolves have used for as long as there have been bison, lynx and wolves. Ecologists objected in unusually blunt terms. The wall was built anyway.

It is worth saying plainly what this place is for a traveller. It is not scenic in the way a fjord is scenic. You walk slowly, in a small group, being told what you are looking at, and the interest is almost entirely in understanding rather than in seeing. That is a specific kind of visit and not everybody wants it.

But if you have spent any time at all in European woodland and wondered why it feels so managed, so even-aged, so quiet — this is the comparison that answers the question, and there is no second example.
