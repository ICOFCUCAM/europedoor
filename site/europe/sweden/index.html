The Nordics

Sweden

An archipelago capital, a thousand miles of forest, and a country that would rather you were comfortable than impressed.

Sweden runs from Baltic islands to Arctic tundra, and most of the middle is forest and lake. The cities are calm and well-made; the countryside is where the country keeps its imagination — allemansrätten lets you walk into almost any of it. Two things surprise first-time visitors: how far north the country goes, and how seriously it takes coffee at three in the afternoon.

Getting around

Trains are fast, punctual and expensive unless booked well ahead; the night train to Abisko is worth taking for its own sake. Stockholm's archipelago runs on ferries all summer and half of it shuts in October. Distances north of Sundsvall are American in scale.

When to come

Midsummer, in the third week of June, effectively closes the country and is the best week to be in it if you have somewhere to be invited. Late summer is warm and empty because Swedes are on their own islands. Winter is for Lapland and light installations, not for Stockholm's daylight.

Worth knowing

  • Card everywhere; many places take no cash at all, including some museums and buses.
  • Systembolaget holds the monopoly on strong alcohol and closes on Sundays.
  • The right to roam comes with the duty not to disturb — never within sight of a house, never a fire in a dry summer.

At the table

  • Fika: coffee and a cardamom bun, treated as an appointment rather than a snack
  • Herring in five preparations at any midsummer table
  • Toast Skagen — prawns, dill and roe on fried bread, the country's best restaurant dish
